How this is worked out

  1. The rotator relations. From issue 185, the rotate command needed to reach a bench angle D is 2D − 0.7 in stationary, 2D − 0.7 − EL in vertang, and 2D − 0.7 ± PARANG − EL in posang. The factor of two is the K-mirror — the sky rotates twice as fast as the mechanism — and 0.7° is NIRC2's INSTANGL. Only the stationary and vertang forms have been confirmed on sky.
  2. Why the angle drifts. NIRC2 sits at a Nasmyth focus, so the field rotates with elevation. vertang holds the image fixed to the horizon and so compensates elevation alone; posang holds it fixed on sky and compensates the parallactic angle as well. Either way the bench angle moves while you track, and all you get to choose is where it starts.
  3. Solving for the start. Inverting the relation above gives bench(t) = (C + 0.7 + EL(t) − s·PARANG(t)) / 2 for a fixed command C. The page picks the C that puts the bench on the target angle at the midpoint of the window, so the sequence spends as much time as possible near peak.
  4. Never a negative command. Adding 360° to the command is a whole turn in the doubled sky frame, which is 180° of bench — the same K-mirror orientation. So a negative solution is shifted up by 360° and the page tells you when it has done that. For the same reason the bench angle itself is only defined modulo 180°, and is reported on whichever branch holds your target angle — that keeps the track continuous rather than tearing it in two. show -s ao obrt may read back a value 180° away, or a multiple of it.
  5. Efficiency. The curves are the commissioning measurements of normalized modulation amplitude against IMR angle, digitized from the summary plot — approximate to about ±0.02, and sampled only over 0–150° (0–90° in K). Values in the unsampled stretch back round to 0° are drawn dashed and marked as extrapolated. No 90° periodicity is assumed; the data does not cleanly support it.
  6. Pointing limits are only flagged, not modelled. Keck II's accessible sky is not a simple elevation cut: across azimuths of about 185°–333°, south through west, the telescope runs into the Nasmyth deck and the floor rises to roughly 37°, while elsewhere it is 18° unvignetted and vignetted below that. The upper limit is 89.5°, and stable guiding is not guaranteed for targets transiting above about 85°. The page checks those documented corner values and raises a flag, but the real boundary is a curve — treat a warning as a prompt to go and read the Keck pointing limits, and the absence of one as no guarantee. Nor is any of this a check on whether the target is worth observing: darkness is flagged from the Sun's elevation, but the Moon, the weather and the azimuth wrap are not considered.
  7. Positions. Coordinates are taken as ICRS/J2000, moved by proper motion to the epoch of observation and precessed to the mean equinox of date. Elevation and parallactic angle are computed for Keck (19.8283° N, 155.4783° W, 4160 m). Nutation, aberration and refraction are neglected — all well under 0.02° here, far below the uncertainty in the rotator relations themselves.
